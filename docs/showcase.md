# Worked showcase: can mobile bicycle tune-ups work in Portland?

[← Back to README](../README.md) · [Install](installation.md) · [Quick start](quickstart.md)

> **Transparent demonstration, not a verified founder interview or a real business recommendation.** This page is a worked walkthrough of the skill's **six-stage method**. Founder responses, proposed business, financial inputs, test targets and conversation excerpts are **invented for demonstration only**. External market and competitor references below point to **real sources checked on 2026-09-28**. These public signals cannot establish customer demand, current booking availability, operational costs or future profitability.

**Demo concept:** A solo bicycle mechanic wants to launch an appointment-based, at-home tune-up service in Portland, Oregon. The target buyer is a time-constrained commuter. The founder is considering a flat 140 USD service fee. **All founder statements in this example are hypothetical.**

## Stage 1 — Ask rather than assume

**Skill:** What exactly would your customer be paying to avoid?

**Illustrative founder:** Transporting the bike to a shop, arranging a pickup and losing access to it for several days

**Skill:** Would you repair e-bikes, carry replacement parts, and travel throughout Portland? Which certifications and insurance do you have? How many appointments could you fulfill in a day?

**Illustrative founder:** I'd start with mechanical work, not battery or motor diagnostics. I don't know my realistic appointments per day yet

**Recorded unknowns:** Certification, liability coverage, travel radius, service scope, time per job, actual ability to convert interested customers into paying bookings

## Stage 2 — Express a falsifiable hypothesis

**H — Proposed value hypothesis:** Some Portland commuters will pay enough of a premium for mobile convenience to sustain a profitable one-person bicycle service

**Counter-hypothesis:** Convenient mobile repair is already readily available and an independent mechanic cannot earn enough after travel, labor, acquisition and unpredictable repairs

**Possible early blockers:** E-bike work outside the mechanic's capabilities; inability to obtain appropriate coverage; too many miles between bookings; competition able to bundle appointments more efficiently

## Stage 3 — Collect bounded external evidence

| Observation | Evidence class | Exact interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Portland's 2025 bicycle count found riding broadly steady for two consecutive years and approximately one quarter of observed riders on e-bikes | **F — primary public-sector data** | The city has observable cycling activity; it does **not** measure willingness to pay for the proposed service |
| Velofix has a Portland mobile bicycle-service location serving homes and offices | **F — competitor self-description** | A **direct competitor/substitute** already offers mobile convenience; its current local transaction volume is unknown |
| Left Coast Bicycles lists mobile bike repair in Portland and advertises a workplace tune-up at 135 USD (a displayed promotional price when checked) | **F — competitor advertised price, date-stamped** | A possible **price anchor**, not an average market transaction price; workplace volume, included services and discounts may differ from the proposed offer |
| Mobile convenience will attract enough paying first-time customers at 140 USD | **H — untested** | Cannot be inferred from city rider counts or competing advertised prices |

**Sources checked on 2026-09-28:**

1. Portland Bureau of Transportation, [Bicycle Counts and the 2025 report](https://www.portland.gov/transportation/walking-biking-transit-safety/bicycle-counts) — city counts, scope and limitations
2. [velofix Portland](https://www.velofix.com/locations/portland/) — mobile service presence and scope; no local conversion figures provided
3. [Left Coast Bicycles: Prices](https://www.leftcoastbicycles.com/prices) — advertised workplace price checked on the date above, subject to later change

**What is missing:** Demand among the proposed *paying segment*; local market size; competitor booking volumes; full price-and-scope comparability; actual CAC; local insurance and professional requirements. Research data is not a substitute for checking these directly

## Stage 4 — Show the math, not a made-up forecast

All numbers in the following table are **illustrative scenario inputs, NOT observed Portland industry costs**. Currency: USD. Founder compensation is represented in per-booking labor; fixed monthly overhead excludes a separate founder salary unless explicitly added

| Illustrative input | USD per booking |
|---|---:|
| Price per tune-up | 140 |
| Parts and consumables | 18 |
| Mechanic labor | 35 |
| Travel and vehicle cost | 15 |
| Marketing allocation per booked job | 12 |
| **Contribution per booking** | **60** |

**C — Worked calculation:** 140 − 18 − 35 − 15 − 12 = **60 USD contribution per completed booking**, before any omitted taxes, unexpected damage, insurance deductibles or seasonal effects

With **2400 USD of assumed fixed monthly costs**, illustrative break-even = 2400 / 60 = **40 completed bookings per month**. Across **20 working days**, this means **2 completed bookings per working day**. That is an arithmetic threshold, **not evidence the mechanic can sell or service 40 jobs**

| Monthly bookings (scenario, not forecast) | Contribution after assumed fixed costs |
|---:|---:|
| 20 | 20 × 60 − 2400 = **−1200 USD** |
| 40 | 40 × 60 − 2400 = **0 USD** |
| 60 | 60 × 60 − 2400 = **1200 USD** |

**Sensitivity:** If contribution falls from 60 to 40 USD per booking, break-even rises to 2400 / 40 = **60 bookings per month**. Weather, service mix, canceled bookings, route optimization and supplier cost changes need measurement. The model is incomplete until the founder supplies actual figures

## Stage 5 — Argue *against* the idea

**Skeptical buyer:** "I can use a shop I already trust; why should I pay a mobile premium?"

**Incumbent competitor:** Existing mobile providers may have reputation, service vans, workplace contracts, established schedules and recurring customers

**Financial reviewer:** Even plausible revenue can fail to cover insurance, vehicle downtime, marketing, rework and the owner's time. Real CAC and journey times are currently unknown

**Operator:** A tune-up lasting longer than planned disrupts subsequent appointments; e-bike mechanical scope and spare parts need a clear service policy

**12-month pre-mortem:** The founder books scattered one-off calls, spends too much time driving, underprices complex jobs and cannot earn enough to maintain the van. This is a **hypothetical failure story**, not a claim about an actual business

## Stage 6 — Conditional assessment and a cheap test

**Evidence so far:** Cycling activity exists and direct mobile competitors exist; neither proves unmet demand or viable unit economics for this new entrant. There is **not enough evidence to recommend committing capital to a full operation**

**Proposed test, subject to the founder's consent:**

1. Check qualifications, insurance and scope before offering services
2. Interview at least **10 real people** in the intended segment about recent repairs and concrete switching barriers; this is a **chosen research target**, not a statistically representative sample
3. Show a truthful service offer with real scope, available dates and transparent pricing; count actual qualified requests, not likes or hypothetical willingness to pay
4. Obtain **a small number of genuine, consent-based pilot bookings or refundable deposits** before purchasing a vehicle or expanding the service area
5. Log real service duration, travel mileage, cost, cancellations and revenue for every pilot job, then replace fictional spreadsheet inputs with observations
6. Agree the maximum test spend and realistic success/stop rules **with the founder before running it**. Do not assert a universal conversion target from this demonstration

**Possible next decision:** Proceed to a limited pilot only if qualifications and coverage are resolved and actual customers accept a price that leaves a meaningful contribution after *measured* costs. Otherwise narrow the service area, change the offering, partner with an existing repair shop or stop the test
